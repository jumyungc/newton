Contacts that settle, stack and hold, and what each fix does

When objects touch, a simulator adds damping to soak up energy, like a shock absorber. Our change to Newton's VBD solver has two parts. A third part was tried and left out. Every case below runs in several versions of Newton, so you can see what each part does on its own.

Fix 1: damp only after touchThe damper acts on the compression gained once the surfaces touch, not on the motion just before contact.
Fix 2: solve the damper with the springThe damper is worked out together with the contact's springiness, so the result is right within the usual amount of computation.
Fix 3, left out: grip counts the whole pushLetting friction use the damper's share of the push makes braking exact, but in a robot's grip that share flickers from step to step and grasped objects get thrown. It is left out until a version that takes the push from the previous step is ready.

A robot hand picks up a small cylinder

Close at 1.6 s, lift 50 cm, hold. The camera follows the hand. Half speed.

Fix 2 is what lets the hand hold: with the damper solved together with the spring, the fingers stop pushing back too hard at first touch. Fix 1 alone does not help. Before the friction change the hand held it but let it turn, because Newton had no spin resistance yet.

The same with a capsule

Same arm and motion.

Same pattern: knocked out in Newton today and with fix 1 alone; held still with fix 2 and with our change.

Towers of boxes

Four towers; in the left two the solver works out every box of a tower at the same time, the hardest case. Real time, 8 s.

Fix 1 alone keeps the towers up but they never settle. Fix 2 alone makes them burst apart at once: without fix 1 it damps the motion before touch. Fixes 1 + 2 together, our change, bring them to rest.

Boxes riding on pushed carts

The three carts differ only in damping. White line: where physics says the box should be. Half speed.

Fix 2 is the one that matters: with the damper solved with the spring, damping no longer changes how far the boxes slide.

Sliding to a stop, on a conveyor and on a slope

Back to front: a 100 g and a 10 g box braking on the floor, a box landing on a moving conveyor, two boxes on a gentle slope. White lines: where physics says each box stops. Newton's default material. Half speed.

This is the case our change leaves imperfect. With fixes 1 + 2 the boxes stop 13 % (100 g) and 5 % (10 g) beyond their lines, against 29 % and 134 % in Newton today, and the light box holds on the slope. The rest of the error comes from friction counting only the spring's share of the push while the damper still carries some of the box's weight. All three fixes (bottom right) stop every box on its line, but that third fix is left out, for the reason below.

Why fix 3 is left out

Separate runs of the robot hand with a 20 g cable instead of the cylinder (separate test runs).

Fix 3 lets friction grip with the damper's share of the push as well as the spring's. That is the right physics, and it is what makes the braking boxes stop on their lines. But the damper's share exists only while a contact is being squeezed. In a grip, the held object jitters by micrometres, so that share switches on and off from one step to the next. The friction forces of the two fingers wind up against each other, and the object is thrown.

  • Cable in the hand, GPU runs: Newton today held it 4 of 4, our change 10 of 10, all three fixes 0 of 7 (thrown every time).
  • Same on the CPU from 16 start positions: lost in 5 (Newton today), 1 (our change), 8 (all three).
  • The cylinder with 60 solver iterations, GPU runs: our change good in 10 of 11, all three in 0 of 7 (4 lost).

Two narrower versions of fix 3 were tried and also failed. A version that takes the push from the previous step, as other solvers of this kind do, is the planned follow-up.
